# Ship one curated route, not a planner

The best itinerary sites do two things well: they put one opinionated route in front of the reader, and they show it as a list of days linked to a map. Consumer planners (Wanderlog, Stippl, Polarsteps) are built around a day list next to a map that redraws as stops change. Premium tour operators (Audley, Selective Asia, Steppes) open with a key-facts strip, a sticky map beside collapsible days, honest notes on alternatives at the stop where the choice arises, and a "when to go" panel. Award-winning route stories add one signature move: the map follows the text. For your 16-day Ho Chi Minh City → Hanoi page in December, about two-thirds of the plan is right: the 16-day colour strip, the to-scale SVG map, the day-by-day plans with travel-leg cards, the December weather section, logistics, and the hosted-plus-offline delivery. The part to cut back hard is the route builder. Four interest sliders plus Auto/Must-see/Skip toggles on 11 places, driving a solver that lists every valid combination, is exactly the "regenerate my trip" mechanic users complain about. It also goes against the research on sliders, choice overload and novice decision-makers. Replace it with 3 hand-made presets and 2–3 written either/or swaps. Trim the 6–7-photo carousels to 3–5 captioned photos. Drop the "Add" buttons that only make sense if the solver stays. The result is still clearly more than a landing page, at a fraction of the build and bug risk.

## Every serious planner shares one core: days beside a map

Strip away the extras and the leading planners converge on the same two linked panes. Wanderlog pairs an itinerary list with an interactive map where "you add places, drag them into days, and the map redraws", and reviewers call that single view "best in the category" ([Endless Travel Plans](https://www.endlesstravelplans.com/guides/planning-tools/wanderlog-review)). It also shows **distance and travel time between consecutive stops, with total travel time under each day's heading** ([Wanderlog FAQ](https://wanderlog.com/blog/faq)). Stippl organises the trip by **nights per location** and auto-calculates travel times between stops ([stippl.io](https://www.stippl.io/)). Polarsteps' planner gives each step an arrival date and night count, and lets you pick a transport mode per leg ("train, bus, bike, boat, or on foot") ([Polarsteps planning update](https://news.polarsteps.com/releases/planning-update-2025)). For a multi-city route like yours, the Polarsteps/Stippl model of "stop × nights" plus "leg × mode × time" fits better than Wanderlog's city-day hops.

The layer on top is where the trouble starts. Wanderlog has a 4.9★ App Store rating but **1.8/5 on Trustpilot**, with complaints that "the app gets slower the more you add to it" ([Endless Travel Plans](https://www.endlesstravelplans.com/guides/planning-tools/wanderlog-review)). An aggregated review analysis flags crashes, a "too busy… too many pop-ups" interface, and a web version that "slows to a crawl with long itineraries" ([Onclarity](https://www.onclarity.com/leaderboard/insight/wanderlog)). Stippl reviewers report dates saving to the wrong day and overnight flights importing incorrectly ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/id6443617088)). The extras that cause these problems are reels, photobooks, eSIM, expense splitting and Gmail parsing. A page shared between two friends doesn't need any of them. The paid tiers are telling too: **offline access, route optimisation and export sit behind Pro paywalls** at Wanderlog, Stippl and Tripomatic ([MonkeyTravel](https://monkeytravel.app/blog/wanderlog-vs-mindtrip-2026); [Stippl blog](https://www.stippl.io/blog/stippl-vs-wanderlog); [tripomatic.com](https://tripomatic.com/)). A free offline HTML file therefore gives your friend something the commercial apps charge for.

Customising by interest is the weakest part of these products. Interest and pace inputs act once, when the plan is generated. Examples are Inspirock's interest checkboxes plus a "fast-paced or relaxed" choice, Stippl's "relaxed, cultural, adventurous" trip types, and TripGenie's "compact or leisurely" toggle. The complaints concentrate on the regeneration step. An Inspirock user wrote: "I find it difficult to edit my plan without the program automatically readjusting" ([Wanderlog's Inspirock review](https://wanderlog.com/blog/?p=558)). Afar found that even the best AI planners "at best serve as outlines" ([Afar](https://www.afar.com/magazine/we-tested-ai-travel-planning-apps-here-are-the-3-that-actually-worked?p=23)). Generated routes "may scatter stops… without accounting for transit time" ([MonkeyTravel](https://monkeytravel.app/blog/wanderlog-vs-mindtrip-2026)). Your solver would not hallucinate, because it only combines places you checked yourself. It does share the core problem, though: a small change to one input can reshuffle the whole route without explaining why.

## Tour operators sell one opinionated route and write the alternatives in

The operator pages are the closer model for a "professional" feel, and they are very consistent. Audley's Vietnam page opens with a hero photo and "19 days from £7,430pp". It follows with six one-line highlights, then an "Itinerary idea" split view: **numbered, collapsible day badges on the left and a sticky map on the right, with the current stop's pin highlighted**, plus "Expand all" ([Audley](https://www.audleytravel.com/vietnam/tours/highlights-of-vietnam)). Audley also writes in a rest day ("Enjoy a full day relaxing at the resort"). It gives night counts per stop in an accommodation accordion, and the dashed route line on the map does the orienting work. Lonely Planet Journeys turns its facts strip (duration, price, "customize this trip") into a sticky bar. It also gives transport a ticket-style block ("BY CAR · DEPART: HANOI → ARRIVE: …") ([Lonely Planet](https://www.lonelyplanet.com/journeys/itineraries/d4bc3ce1-d45e-4189-8057-17d7eb3737fc)). Steppes and Rough Guides group days by stop ("Days 3-4: Hanoi - Halong Bay"; "Days 5-8: Sapa region"), and Rough Guides adds a bold "Travel time: 4 hours by bus or private car" line to each stop ([Steppes](https://www.steppestravel.com/holiday-ideas/highlights-of-vietnam/); [Rough Guides](https://roughguides.com/vietnam/itineraries/14-days-in-vietnam-itinerary)).

The key lesson for your route builder is how these operators handle variation. They show **one complete route framed as a starting point**: "Itinerary idea" (Audley), "BESPOKE HOLIDAY IDEA" (Steppes), "Think of this itinerary as a starting point rather than a fixed package" (Insight Guides) ([Insight Guides](https://www.insightguides.com/destinations/asia-pacific/vietnam/trips/winter-trip/)). Choices appear only where they matter, and each comes with an honest trade-off. Selective Asia calls Ha Long "a conundrum… Hundreds of boats ply the waters" and recommends Bai Tu Long or Lan Ha ("slightly quieter… but it will still feel busy!"). It also suggests the rural north as an alternative ([Selective Asia](https://www.selectiveasia.com/vietnam-holidays/journeys/top-to-tail/)). Exodus offers a "paved flat route" or a "slightly more demanding scenic route" inside a single hiking day ([Exodus](https://www.exodus.co.uk/trips/vietnam-holidays/culture/vietnam-uncovered/aos)). Sibling routes appear as a short row of 3–6 cards at the bottom. Effort is summarised by **a single activity label, not a filter panel**: "Physical Grading: 2 - Light" ([G Adventures](https://www.gadventures.com/trips/vietnam-roadtrip-hanoi-to-ho-chi-minh-city/AVHH/)). No operator observed lets the reader toggle an alternative and watch the map update live. That proves nothing either way about whether live toggles work. It does mean an in-page solver has no established convention behind it, so you would be inventing the interaction yourself.

There are two areas where operator pages are weak, and your plan already covers both. **Transport between stops is mostly buried in prose**: Audley's day chips show "Mekong Delta → Phu Quoc" but give no duration, and no observed Vietnam page shows every leg as mode + time ([Audley](https://www.audleytravel.com/vietnam/tours/highlights-of-vietnam)). Lonely Planet's own page contradicts itself on one leg ("roughly three-hour drive" vs "roughly three and a half hours") ([Lonely Planet](https://www.lonelyplanet.com/journeys/itineraries/d4bc3ce1-d45e-4189-8057-17d7eb3737fc)). Your travel-leg cards fill a real gap. **Seasonality is either a single header line or a widget that tells two stories at once.** Audley's December verdict reads "The best time to travel". Its own per-stop bars for that month show **Hue at 329 mm and Hoi An at 214 mm of rain**, against Hanoi at 22 °C/20 mm and Ho Chi Minh City at 31 °C/44 mm ([Audley](https://www.audleytravel.com/vietnam/tours/highlights-of-vietnam)). Its climate table puts **Sapa at a 16 °C daily maximum** in December ([Audley best time](https://www.audleytravel.com/vietnam/best-time-to-visit)). Selective Asia warns that Sapa "can get very cold and frosty in December" and that Dalat is drier but "cold in December & January" ([Selective Asia weather](https://www.selectiveasia.com/vietnam-holidays/weather/)). A December HCMC → Hanoi page that shows these figures per stop is more honest than most professional pages.

The operators also show what to avoid. Audley illustrates its Hoi An day with northern rice-terrace photos and captions three Mekong images "Delta scene". Bamba lists a trip "From $1,655,366,484.00 USD" ([Audley](https://www.audleytravel.com/vietnam/tours/highlights-of-vietnam); [Bamba](https://bambatravel.com/landings/countries/vietnam)). On these pages, a premium feel comes from correct photos, named stays and a candid voice, not from photo volume.

## Research favours presets over sliders, visible content over hidden content, and fewer photos

The UX evidence sharpens the operator lessons into rules. A meta-analysis of 53 studies finds that choice overload is strongest under **preference uncertainty and a complex product** ([Kellogg](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/2015/when-product-assortment-leads-to-choice-overload-a-conceptual)). Two friends planning an unfamiliar country, with a bundled 16-day trip, match both conditions. A field experiment with 1.6 million shoppers found purchase likelihood **rose and then fell** as the number of recommendations grew. **Up to 64% of the drop came from people never starting to engage** ([INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/msom.2022.0659)). So the first screen has to be useful with no setup at all. Showing a single option is not ideal either. The most directly relevant study is Hildebrand, Häubl and Herrmann: across nine studies, **vacation packages** among them, picking one of a few pre-made "starting solutions" and then refining it raised satisfaction and lowered perceived complexity compared with attribute-by-attribute configuration ([University of St. Gallen](https://www.alexandria.unisg.ch/236290)). Knijnenburg et al. found **novices did better with a simple popular-items recommender** than with attribute-weighting controls ([ACM](https://unpaywall.org/10.1145%2F2043932.2043960)).

Sliders do especially poorly. Nielsen Norman Group (NN/g) says sliders suit only approximate values and are hard to use on touch screens, particularly one-handed ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/gui-slider-controls/)). WCAG 2.2 requires a non-drag alternative for anything operated by dragging ([W3C 2.5.7](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/dragging.html)). The tested alternative is buttons. MovieLens users given simple "more/less popular" buttons rated their adjusted lists as a better fit ([GroupLens](https://grouplens.org/blog/putting-users-in-control-of-their-recommendations/)). Tourism researchers also argue that **explicit preference ratings are unreliable early in trip planning**, because people read destination features differently ([CEUR-WS](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-1685/paper5.pdf)). A "hiking = 2 of 3" setting asks two first-time visitors to rate something they cannot yet judge.

Hidden content follows the same pattern. NN/g eyetracking puts **about 57% of viewing time above the fold and 74% in the first two screens** ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)). When map and list sit side by side, **95% of users engage with the map**. On list-only sites, 65% never use it ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/accommodations-split-view)). The NYT's Archie Tse put it bluntly: "readers just want to scroll". If you make readers do anything else, "something spectacular has to happen" ([Nieman Lab](https://www.niemanlab.org/2016/03/at-the-malofiej-infographics-world-summit-the-best-form-of-storytelling-is-often-static/)). Gregor Aisch's counterpoint is that "15% of readers aren't nobody" ([vis4.net](http://www.vis4.net/blog/in-defense-of-interactive-graphics/)). Optional controls are fine, then, as long as nothing essential sits behind them. NN/g caps useful progressive disclosure at **two levels** ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/)). It also finds that sticky, collapsible tables of contents often go unnoticed on mobile, so a day index in the body of the page works better ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/table-of-contents/)).

Carousels are where the plan departs most from the evidence. NN/g recommends **five or fewer frames**, a counter rather than dots (mobile users don't notice dots), no auto-advance, and a peek of the next image ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/designing-effective-carousels/)). Baymard found 50% of desktop users missed extra images when only dots were shown, and recommends thumbnails plus descriptive text ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/always-use-thumbnails-additional-images)). Users also expect pinch and double-tap zoom ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/mobile-image-gestures)). WCAG adds Previous/Next buttons as an alternative to swiping and touch targets of at least 24×24 px ([Hidde de Vries](https://hidde.blog/new-in-wcag22/)). Six or seven photos per place across ten or more places means 60–80 images. That is past the point where readers keep looking, and it makes the offline file very large.

Award-winning route stories add one positive pattern and a list of things to avoid. The positive pattern is the **map that follows the text**: WaPo's "Safe Passages" route animates as you scroll, the SF Chronicle traces a 49-mile scenic route, and Huttrip syncs an elevation graph to the trail ([Mapbox](https://www.mapbox.com/blog/8-compelling-stories-told-with-the-storytelling-solution)). The things to avoid are scrolljacking, which disoriented "the majority of our study participants" ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolljacking-101/)); large pans and zooms without a `prefers-reduced-motion` fallback ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/@media/prefers-reduced-motion)); and sticky scrolly layouts on phones, where The Pudding advises to "stack it" ([The Pudding](https://pudding.cool/process/responsive-scrollytelling/)). The Webby criteria fit a friends' page better than the Awwwards ones: clear voice, transparent navigation, fast loading, and "return visits, bookmarking, sharing" ([Webby](https://www.webbyawards.com/judging-criteria/)).

## Verdict on the seven-part plan: keep five, rebuild one, drop one

The table below takes each element of the proposal in turn. The guiding test comes from the evidence above. Keep an element if it shows information that is otherwise hard to see: the trip's shape, the legs, the weather. Cut or simplify it if it asks two first-time visitors to configure something they cannot yet judge.

| # | Proposed element | Verdict | What to change and why |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Route builder: 4–5 presets, 4 interest sliders (0–3), Auto/Must-see/Skip on 11 places, solver enumerating all valid 16-day routes | **Rebuild** | Keep **3 presets**, hand-built and clearly different (e.g. "Balanced", "More hiking", "Slower + beach"), backed by the starting-solutions and diversity evidence. **Cut the sliders** (touch, WCAG and novice evidence). **Cut the 11 three-way toggles and the solver.** Together they allow 3¹¹ ≈ 177,000 toggle states before the sliders are counted, which is the "automatically readjusting" problem in another form. Replace them with **2–3 either/or swaps** at real decision points (e.g. central coast in the rain vs extra highland days; Ha Long vs Lan Ha/Ninh Bình), each with a two-column trade-off. A swap may update the strip and map, but it changes one block, never the whole trip. |
| 2 | Live summary: 16-day colour strip, flights, road hours, "why this route" line | **Keep** | This is the highest-value, lowest-cost element. Make the strip **double as the day index** (each cell links to `#day-07`), and mark travel days and nights. Keep "why this route" to **one sentence tied to the four interests**, which serves the transparency aim. |
| 3 | Sticky to-scale SVG route map that redraws per route | **Keep, adapt for mobile** | Sticky split view on desktop (Audley pattern; 95% map engagement). On phones, **stack it**: one map near the top with numbered pins matching the day numbers, and no sticky panel covering the text. Highlight the active stop as the reader scrolls, step by step rather than continuously, with a static fallback under reduced motion. Redrawing for 3 presets is cheap. |
| 4 | Per-place carousels (6–7 photos, swipe, arrows, lightbox), day plans, hidden-gem and skip→instead tips, travel-leg cards | **Keep, trim the photos** | **3–5 photos per stop**, with the most representative first, a "2/5" counter, captions naming the exact place, Previous/Next buttons of at least 24 px, a zoomable lightbox and no autoplay. Group days under stop headings ("Days 6–8 · Đà Lạt"), one line per day plus one level of detail. Keep tips short and candid (Selective Asia voice). **Leg cards are a differentiator**: mode icon, approximate hours, an "eats a day/night" flag, and a Google Maps link. |
| 5 | "Not on this route" section with Add buttons | **Change** | The Add buttons only make sense with a solver. Turn this into a **short card row of 3–5 places** you deliberately left out, each with one honest line on why and what it would replace. This copies the operators' sibling-route row and keeps the decision in the friends' conversation. |
| 6 | December weather and logistics (flights, packing, booking) | **Keep, make weather per stop** | Per-stop °C/rain bars for December only, and a per-stop verdict instead of a blanket "best time". Name the wet central coast and the cold highland and Sapa nights plainly. Tie the packing list to those bars. Keep the flight list and a short booking checklist; Audley advises booking before the Christmas period. |
| 7 | Hosted page + single offline HTML via WhatsApp | **Keep both** | Offline access is paywalled in the leading apps, so this is real value. Use an inline SVG map (no tiles, no API keys), compress the embedded photos, add a print stylesheet, and give the hosted link an og:image of about 1200×630 px kept small for the WhatsApp preview. |

Two additions are justified by the evidence and cost little. The first is a **"copy our picks" button** that turns the chosen preset and swaps into a short text (or a URL with the state in the hash) to paste back into WhatsApp. Without a backend, choices stored on one phone never reach the other, and the group-decision research finds that **discussion, not vote aggregation, drives satisfaction**: more than two-thirds of people whose first choice lost were still satisfied ([CEUR-WS](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-1685/paper5.pdf)). The second is a **per-day pace tag** ("Active hike", "Easy culture", "Rest day"). It does the job the sliders were meant to do, but as information rather than as a control, in the same way Exodus and G Adventures use one activity label instead of filters.

Some uncertainty remains. None of the sources tested an itinerary page specifically, and none compared live swap toggles with written alternatives. The ranking therefore rests on adjacent evidence: e-commerce galleries, hotel map/list layouts, recommender studies, and destination-choice experiments in which only 7 of 55 groups were two people. The research did not cover how WhatsApp's in-app browser handles a locally saved HTML file or local storage, so test the offline file on both friends' phones before relying on it. December figures for Đà Lạt, Phong Nha, Ninh Bình and Cát Bà were not in the sources, so those bars need another climate source.

## A prioritised feature set that stays simple

| Priority | Feature | Why it earns its place |
|---|---|---|
| **Must-have** | Hero photo with a "where is this" caption and a facts strip (16 days · HCMC → Hanoi · December · nights/flights/hiking days) | Most attention falls on the first screen, and it must be useful with zero setup |
| **Must-have** | 16-day colour strip that doubles as the day index, plus a one-line "why this route" | Shows the trip's shape and pace at a glance; makes each day linkable |
| **Must-have** | Inline SVG route map with numbered stops (sticky beside the list on desktop, stacked on mobile; flights dashed, ground legs solid) | The universal core of planners and operators; side-by-side placement drives map use |
| **Must-have** | Days grouped under stop headings, one line per day, one level of detail, pace tag per day | Supports skimming by headings; two disclosure levels at most |
| **Must-have** | Travel-leg cards between stops (mode, hours, overnight flag, Maps link) | Few operator pages do this well; prevents the date-and-transit mistakes planners make |
| **Must-have** | December weather per stop (°C + rain bars, honest verdict, what to pack) | The north/central/south split is the trip's biggest planning risk |
| **Must-have** | Flights list and short booking checklist | Practical information the friends will come back to |
| **Must-have** | Hosted link + offline single HTML file + print CSS | Works on the road; offline is a paid feature elsewhere |
| **Nice-to-have** | 3 hand-built presets ("Balanced / More hiking / Slower + beach") that update strip, map and days | Starting solutions beat configuration; few, clearly different options |
| **Nice-to-have** | 2–3 either/or swaps with two-column trade-offs and a Plan B | Gives the friends something concrete to discuss |
| **Nice-to-have** | 3–5-photo galleries per stop with counter, buttons and zoomable lightbox | Emotional pull without carousel blindness or file bloat |
| **Nice-to-have** | "Left out on purpose" card row; "copy our picks" text/URL; mini elevation profile on hike days | Low-cost aids to the discussion and to expectations |
| **Leave out** | Interest sliders, per-place Auto/Must/Skip toggles, combination solver, Add buttons | Overload, touch and accessibility problems, unpredictable reshuffles, heavy build and test load |
| **Leave out** | Scrolljacking, 3D/WebGL maps or globes, autoplay video or carousels, tile-based maps, hover-only details | Most-criticised patterns; heavy on mobile data and offline file size |
| **Leave out** | Budget splitting, accounts, comments, reviews, pop-ups | App bloat and sales clutter with no value for two friends |

## Conclusion

Your plan correctly sees that the page should be interactive. It puts the interactivity in the wrong place. The tools users praise make the route visible: the strip, the map, the legs, the weather. The tools they complain about make the route variable through engines that recompute it. Treat the presets as editorial choices you have already made and annotated, not as outputs of an algorithm. The page then becomes what no commercial product offers: an itinerary checked for one route in one month, with the central-coast rain and the cold northern nights stated plainly, and it works offline without an account.

The practical consequence is that the hardest engineering in the plan, the solver and its 11-place state space, adds the least value. Moving that effort into accurate leg times, per-stop December data and correctly captioned photos produces a page that feels more professional, loads faster from a WhatsApp link, and leaves the actual decision where the research says it belongs: in the conversation between the two friends.
